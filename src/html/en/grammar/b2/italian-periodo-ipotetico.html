The chart: every type in one table

An if-sentence has two parts: the condition (with se, “if”) and the result. The tenses tell you how likely the idea is: from almost certain to impossible. The logic is the same as English first, second and third conditionals.

The golden rule: never a conditional after se, just like English never says “if I would”. The conditional goes in the result; after se you use the indicative (real) or the subjunctive (possible and unreal).

Italian if-clauses compared with English

Italian types match the English first, second and third conditionals. The difference: after “if” English uses a past tense (if I had), Italian uses the congiuntivo (se avessi).

EnglishItalian
If it rains, I’ll stay at home.Se piove, resto a casa.
If I had time, I would read more.Se avessi tempo, leggerei di più.
If I were you, I’d talk to her.Se fossi in te, parlerei con lei.
If I had known, I would have come.Se l’avessi saputo, sarei venuto.
If I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now.Se avessi studiato medicina, ora sarei medico.
  • “If I had” is se avessi, not se avevo: the past tense after “if” becomes the subjunctive.
  • “If I were you” is se fossi in te: Italian keeps the subjunctive alive where English keeps only “were”.
  • “Would have” becomes avrei or sarei, chosen as in the passato prossimo.

Real: se + indicative

When the condition is real or very likely, both parts take the indicative, as in the English first conditional. The imperative also works in the result.

Present + present: things that always happen
Se piove, resto a casa. · Se scaldi il ghiaccio, si scioglie.
Future + future: something in the future
Se verrai a Roma, ti porterò al Colosseo.
Present + imperative: advice
Se hai fame, mangia qualcosa!
With the passato prossimo
Se hai finito, possiamo uscire. · Se Marco ha detto così, è vero.

Careful: English says “if you come, I will take you”, with the present after “if”. Spoken Italian also prefers the present (se vieni, ti porto), but if you use the future, use it in both parts: se verrai, ti porterò.

Possible, and unreal in the present

For a condition that is only possible, or untrue now, Italian uses se + congiuntivo imperfetto and the condizionale presente in the result: the English “if I had…, I would…”. The form is the same for both; the meaning tells you whether it can happen.

Possible: it could happen
Se domani avessi tempo, verrei con voi.
Unreal now: it isn’t so
Se fossi un uccello, volerei sopra il mare. · Se abitassi a Napoli, mangerei la pizza ogni giorno.
Se fossi in te: “if I were you”, to give advice
Se fossi in te, parlerei con lei.

You need the congiuntivo imperfetto (avessi, fossi, facessi, potessi) and the condizionale presente (avrei, sarei, farei, potrei). “If I were” is se fossi: English keeps a trace of the subjunctive here too.

Unreal in the past

For something that did not happen, Italian uses se + congiuntivo trapassato and the condizionale passato: the English third conditional, “if I had known, I would have…”. It is the sentence of regrets and missed chances.

With avere
Se avessi saputo la risposta, l’avrei detta.
With essere: the participle agrees
Se fossi partito prima, sarei arrivato in tempo.
A missed chance
Se avessimo prenotato, avremmo trovato posto.
The result can come first
Ti avrei aiutato volentieri, se me l’avessi chiesto.

The forms are explained in the lessons on the congiuntivo passato and trapassato (avessi fatto, fossi andato) and the condizionale passato (avrei fatto, sarei andato). Where English always says “had” and “would have”, Italian chooses between avere and essere as in the passato prossimo.

The mixed type: the past changes the present

Sometimes the condition and the result belong to two different times. Then the types mix, exactly like English mixed conditionals: each part takes the tense it needs.

Past → now: congiuntivo trapassato + condizionale presente
Se avessi studiato medicina, ora sarei medico. · Se ieri sera fossi andato a letto presto, oggi non sarei stanco.
Always → past: congiuntivo imperfetto + condizionale passato
Se fossi più attento, non avresti perso le chiavi. · Se Laura parlasse tedesco, avrebbe accettato quel lavoro a Berlino.

Ask yourself two questions. When would the condition have happened? In the past: avessi studiato (if I had studied); in general or now: fossi (if I were). When is the result? Now: sarei (I would be); in the past: avrei perso (I would have lost). Words like ora, adesso, oggi, ieri, l’anno scorso help a lot.

In speech: se lo sapevo, venivo

In conversation many Italians use the imperfetto indicativo in both parts for the unreal past. It is quicker and lighter, a bit like English “if I knew, I’d have come” in casual speech.

Spoken: imperfetto + imperfetto
Se lo sapevo, venivo. · Se partivi prima, non perdevi il treno.
Written and careful Italian
Se l’avessi saputo, sarei venuto. · Se fossi partito prima, non avresti perso il treno.

Se lo sapevo, venivo is normal among friends, in messages and in films, but don’t write it in an essay, a work email or an exam: there you need se l’avessi saputo, sarei venuto. Learn the careful form and recognise the spoken one. You will also hear the mixed se lo sapevo, sarei venuto: that is spoken only, too.

Other ways to express a condition

You don’t always need se: Italian has other words and structures, as English has “in case”, “provided that”, “otherwise”.

The gerund
Studiando un po’ ogni giorno, migliorerai presto.
Nel caso in cui, qualora (in case, should) + subjunctive
Nel caso in cui piovesse, la festa si farà in casa. · Qualora ci fossero problemi, chiamateci.
A patto che, purché (provided that) + subjunctive
Ti presto la macchina, a patto che tu me la riporti stasera. · Puoi uscire, purché tu torni per cena.
Altrimenti (otherwise), al tuo posto (in your place)
Sbrigati, altrimenti perdiamo il treno. · Al tuo posto, accetterei.

Nel caso in cui, qualora, a patto che, purché always take the subjunctive, even when the idea is likely. Qualora is formal, like “should there be…”: you find it in contracts and notices.

The traps

Three mistakes that advanced students make too.

After se: never the conditional
Se avessi tempo… (mai: se avrei tempo)
The future: in both parts or in neither
Se domani piove, resto a casa. · Se domani pioverà, resterò a casa.
“Whether” se is not an if-clause
Non so se verrà. · Mi chiedo se abbia capito.

Se avrei is the most famous mistake, even among Italians: it is like saying “if I would have”. But note: non so se verrà (I don’t know whether he will come) is not an if-clause. Here se means “whether”, and the future or the conditional can follow it (mi chiedevo se avresti potuto aiutarmi).

Typical mistakes English speakers make

Wrong: Se avevo tempo, leggerei. Correct: Se avessi tempo, leggerei.
“If I had time”: after se use the subjunctive, avessi.
Wrong: Se sarei in te, parlerei con lei. Correct: Se fossi in te, parlerei con lei.
“If I were you”: se fossi in te.
Wrong: Se avrei saputo, sarei venuto. Correct: Se avessi saputo, sarei venuto.
“If I had known”, never “if I would have known”: se avessi saputo.
