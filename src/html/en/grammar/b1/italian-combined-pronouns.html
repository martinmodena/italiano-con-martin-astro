Italian combined pronouns

Ti do il regalo? Te lo do. “I’ll give it to you” in Italian is two pronouns glued before the verb, in the opposite order to English: first to whom, then what. The rule

When a sentence has two pronouns, an indirect one (to whom?) and a direct one (what?), they go together: indirect first, direct second, the opposite of “give it to me”. And the indirect pronoun changes its i to e.

First to whom, then what
Ti do il regalo. → Te lo do.
The i becomes e
mi → me · ti → te · ci → ce · vi → ve
In questions too
Mi mandi la foto? → Me la mandi?

Look at the photo: ti do il regalo → te lo do (I’ll give it to you). Te = “to you”, lo = “it” (the present). Before this lesson, make sure you know direct pronouns and indirect pronouns.

Combined pronouns compared with English

In English pronouns follow the verb and the thing comes first: give it to me. In Italian they go before the verb and the person comes first: me lo dai.

EnglishItalian
I’ll give it to you.Te lo do.
Give it to me!Dammelo!
I told him / her / them.Gliel’ho detto.
Can you send it to me?Me lo puoi mandare? · Puoi mandarmelo?
Give me two of them!Dammene due!
  • First to whom, then what: te lo (to you + it), never lo ti.
  • Him, her, them + it are all glielo.
  • “Of them” with a quantity is ne: dammene due.

All forms are two words, except those with gli and le, which become one word: glielo, gliela, glieli, gliele, gliene. Si becomes se: se lo mette (he puts it on), se ne va (he leaves).

Glielo for everyone

With gli (to him) and le (to her) the form is the same: glie- + direct pronoun. In speech it also covers “to them”.

To him
Do il libro a Marco. → Glielo do.
To her
Do il libro ad Anna. → Glielo do.
To them (spoken)
Do il libro ai ragazzi. → Glielo do.
To you (polite Lei)
Signora, Le porto il conto? → Glielo porto subito.

Glielo do can mean “I give it to him / her / them / you (polite)”: context makes it clear. For “to them”, formal writing also has lo do loro, but people say glielo do. Polite: Glielo porto subito, signora (I’ll bring it to you right away).

With ne

Ne replaces a quantity (“of them”, “some”) or “about it”. With an indirect pronoun it behaves like lo: me ne, te ne, gliene, ce ne, ve ne.

How many? With a number
Mi dai due biscotti? → Me ne dai due?
A bit, a lot, some
Ti porto un po’ d’acqua. → Te ne porto un po’.
Gliene
Compro tre penne a Luca. → Gliene compro tre.
Ne = about it
Ti ho parlato del viaggio? → Te ne ho parlato?

The number or amount stays: me ne dai due? (can you give me two of them?), gliene compro tre. Ne also replaces “about / of + something”: ti ho parlato del viaggio? → te ne ho parlato? (did I tell you about it?). With “there are”: ce ne sono molti (there are many of them).

In the past

In the passato prossimo lo and la take an apostrophe before avere, and the past participle agrees with the direct pronoun. English has nothing like this.

Me l’ha dato: masculine
Chi ti ha dato il libro? Me l’ha dato Marco.
Gliel’ho mandata: feminine
Hai mandato la foto a Giulia? Sì, gliel’ho mandata.
Me li hanno restituiti: plural
Ti hanno restituito i soldi? Sì, me li hanno restituiti.
Me ne ha mandate tre: with ne
Quante foto ti ha mandato? Me ne ha mandate tre.

The participle takes the gender and number of the thing: te l’ho dato (the book), te l’ho data (the key), te li ho dati, te le ho date. With ne it agrees with the quantity: me ne ha mandate tre (photos). In speech the agreement with ne is sometimes dropped, but with lo, la, li, le it’s compulsory. See the passato prossimo.

Where do they go?

Usually before the verb. With the infinitive, the gerund and the imperative they are attached to the end of the verb.

Before the verb
Te lo dico domani.
Infinitive: attached, or before the modal
Voglio dirtelo. · Te lo voglio dire.
Imperative: attached
Dammelo! · Diglielo! · Fammi vedere → Fammelo vedere!
Negative imperative: both work
Non dirmelo! · Non me lo dire!
Gerund: attached
Spiegandoglielo, ho capito anch’io.

With potere, volere, dovere, sapere you have two options: voglio dirtelo or te lo voglio dire (I want to tell you), never voglio te lo dire. The infinitive drops its final e: dire → dirtelo. After da’, di’, fa’, sta’, va’ the consonant doubles: dammelo (give it to me), diglielo, fammelo (but gli doesn’t double). See the imperative.

With reflexives

Reflexive pronouns combine too. Where English says “I wash my hands”, Italian says mi lavo le mani, and then me le lavo (I wash them).

Me le lavo
Mi lavo le mani. → Me le lavo.
Si → se
Marco si mette la giacca. → Se la mette.
In the past with essere
Ti sei messo il cappotto? Sì, me lo sono messo.

With body parts and clothes, Italian uses a reflexive instead of “my”: mi metto il cappotto → me lo metto (I put it on), si lava i capelli → se li lava. In the past the auxiliary stays essere and the participle agrees with the direct pronoun: me le sono lavate (my hands). See reflexive verbs.

Set phrases

Some very common verbs already contain two pronouns. Learn them as whole words.

Avercela: I’ve got it
Hai la patente? Sì, ce l’ho. · No, non ce l’ho.
Andarsene: I’m off
È tardi, me ne vado.
Farcela: I can make it
È difficile, ma ce la faccio!
Sentirsela: I don’t feel up to it
Sono stanco, non me la sento di uscire.
Prendersela: don’t take it badly
Non te la prendere, scherzavo!

Ce l’ho is the normal way to say “I’ve got it” in answers (hai la penna? Sì, ce l’ho). Me ne vado = I’m leaving. Ce la faccio = I can do it, I’ll make it. Non me la sento = I don’t feel up to it. Prendersela = to get upset: non te la prendere (don’t take it personally).

Traps

Four points where many learners slip.

Mi, ti, ci, vi become me, te, ce, ve
me lo, te la, ce li (mai mi lo, ti la)
Glie- is joined on
glielo, gliela, gliene: una parola sola
The order never changes
te lo do (mai lo ti do)
In the past the participle agrees
Le chiavi? Te le ho date.

Say me lo, never mi lo; glielo, never gli lo. The indirect pronoun always comes first: te lo do, not lo ti do, even though English says “give it to you”. And in the past the participle follows the thing: le chiavi? Te le ho date.

Typical mistakes English speakers make

Wrong: Lo ti do. Correct: Te lo do.
I give it to you: in Italian the person comes first, te lo.
Wrong: Dai me lo! Correct: Dammelo!
Give it to me! → dammelo, one word.
Wrong: Gli lo dico. Correct: Glielo dico.
I tell him + it → glielo.
Wrong: La chiave? Te l’ho dato. Correct: La chiave? Te l’ho data.
The participle agrees with la chiave: data.
Wrong: Dammi due. Correct: Dammene due.
Give me two (of them): you need ne.
