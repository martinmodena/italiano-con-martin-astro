The Italian condizionale presente: vorrei, potresti, dovresti

Vorrei un caffè, potresti aiutarmi?, dovresti dormire di più: the Italian present conditional is how you ask politely, give advice, talk about wishes and imagine. Its uses, the form, the irregular verbs (sarei, andrei, vorrei), useful phrases for the bar and the shop, advice and the traps. With 30 exercises.

When to use it

The conditional is the mood of what you would, could or should do. Where English adds “would”, “could” or “should” to a verb, Italian changes the ending of the verb itself. Italians use it all the time, above all to be polite.

To ask politely
Vorrei un caffè, per favore.
For a polite request
Potresti chiudere la finestra?
To give advice
Dovresti dormire di più.
For a wish
Mi piacerebbe vivere al mare.
To imagine
Con più tempo, imparerei il giapponese.
For unconfirmed news
Secondo i giornali, il ministro sarebbe a Parigi.

Voglio un caffè (“I want a coffee”) is correct but sounds blunt, almost an order; vorrei un caffè (“I’d like a coffee”) is what everybody says at the bar. In newspapers the conditional marks news that is not yet certain: il ministro sarebbe a Parigi means “the minister is reportedly in Paris”.

The conditional compared with English

English puts would, could, should before the verb. Italian packs it all into one word: I would go → andrei, could you → potresti, you should → dovresti.

EnglishItalian
I'd like a coffee.Vorrei un caffè.
Could you help me?Potresti aiutarmi?
You should sleep more.Dovresti dormire di più.
If I had time, I would travel.Se avessi tempo, viaggerei.
Every summer we would go to the sea.Ogni estate andavamo al mare.
  • After se (if) Italian uses the subjunctive, not the conditional: se avessi tempo, not se avrei tempo.
  • Would for past habits is not the conditional: andavamo (imperfetto).
  • I'd like → vorrei is the most natural way to ask in a bar or a shop.

The form

Start from the same stem as the future: drop the final -e of the infinitive (in -are verbs the a becomes e) and add the endings -ei, -esti, -ebbe, -emmo, -este, -ebbero, the same for all verbs. One word does the job of English “would” + verb: parlerei = “I would speak”.

Irregular verbs

Three groups to remember. They lose the vowel: andrei, avrei, potrei, dovrei, saprei, vedrei, vivrei. They have a double r: vorrei, verrei, terrei, rimarrei, berrei. They keep the a: farei, darei, starei. And essere becomes sarei.

Useful phrases

At the bar, in a shop, on the phone and among friends the conditional is the normal form of politeness. These phrases are worth learning by heart.

At the bar or restaurant
Vorrei un cappuccino e un cornetto, per favore.
In a shop, with Lei
Scusi, avrebbe questa maglia in blu?
On the phone
Buongiorno, potrei parlare con la dottoressa Bianchi?
To ask a stranger a favour
Le dispiacerebbe aprire il finestrino?
To suggest something to a friend
Ti andrebbe di venire al cinema stasera?

With people you don’t know Italians use Lei, so the third person: potrebbe, avrebbe, Le dispiacerebbe (“would you mind”). With a friend: potresti, ti dispiacerebbe. Ti andrebbe di…? means “would you like to…?” / “do you fancy…?” and is the most natural way to invite someone.

Advice and opinions

To give advice without sounding like a teacher, the conditional is perfect: it suggests, it doesn’t order. It matches English “you should”, “you could”, “I would”.

Dovresti: direct advice (“you should”)
Dovresti bere più acqua.
Potresti: a possibility (“you could”)
Potresti chiedere a Licia: lei lo sa.
Al posto tuo: “if I were you”
Al posto tuo, prenderei il treno.
Sarebbe meglio + infinitive: “it would be better to”
Sarebbe meglio partire presto.
Direi che: a soft opinion (“I’d say”)
Direi che è una buona idea.

Al posto tuo or io + conditional (io prenderei il treno, “I’d take the train”) is the most common piece of advice. You can also say se fossi in te, prenderei il treno: that is an if-clause. With direi, penserei, preferirei an opinion sounds gentler: direi di sì, “I’d say yes”.

The traps

Five points where almost everybody goes wrong, even at an advanced level.

Andremo (future) or andremmo (conditional)?
Domani andremo al mare. · Andremmo al mare, ma piove.
After se: subjunctive, not conditional
Se avessi tempo, viaggerei.
Vorrei + infinitive or vorrei che + subjunctive
Vorrei venire. · Vorrei che tu venissi.
Piacerebbe or piacerebbero?
Mi piacerebbe un gelato. · Mi piacerebbero due gelati.
Today or yesterday? Present or past conditional
Oggi verrei volentieri. · Ieri sarei venuto volentieri.

One m or two? andremo is future (“we will go”), andremmo is conditional (“we would go”): you can hear the double m, so hold it longer. After se (if) never use the conditional, just as English never says “if I would have”: se avessi, with the imperfect subjunctive. With vorrei, if the subject stays the same use the infinitive; if it changes you need che + imperfect subjunctive: vorrei che tu venissi, “I’d like you to come”. Piacere agrees with the thing you like, as the lesson on the verb piacere explains. And for a wish that didn’t come true in the past (“I would have come”) you need the past conditional: sarei venuto.

Typical mistakes English speakers make

Wrong: Se avrei tempo, viaggerei. Correct: Se avessi tempo, viaggerei.
If I had: after se you need the subjunctive, never the conditional.
Wrong: Da bambino andrei al mare ogni estate. Correct: Da bambino andavo al mare ogni estate.
We would go for a past habit is the imperfetto, not the conditional.
Wrong: Noi andremo volentieri, ma piove. Correct: Noi andremmo volentieri, ma piove.
We would go: conditional with double m; andremo means we will go.
Wrong: Io anderei in treno. Correct: Io andrei in treno.
Andare uses the future stem andr-: andrei.
Wrong: Voglio un caffè, per favore. Correct: Vorrei un caffè, per favore.
I want sounds blunt: at the bar say vorrei, I’d like.
