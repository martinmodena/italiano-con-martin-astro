When to use it

The imperative is for asking someone to do (or not do) something: giving instructions, inviting, advising, forbidding. In Italian it isn’t rude: the tone comes from your voice and from words like per favore and pure. English has one imperative for everybody (“speak!”); Italian changes it depending on who you talk to.

Instructions and directions
Gira a destra e poi vai sempre dritto.
An invitation
Entra pure, accomodati!
Advice
Dormi un po’, sei stanco.
A ban
Non toccare, è caldo!
A recipe, written instructions
Tagliate le cipolle e aggiungete l’olio.
A suggestion with noi (“let’s”)
Andiamo al mare!

Pure makes the imperative friendlier: entra pure, si sieda pure mean “go ahead, feel free”. Recipes and instructions often use voi (tagliate, aggiungete) or the infinitive (tagliare, aggiungere).

The imperative compared with English

English has one imperative for one person or many: speak! Italian has two: parla (one person, tu) and parlate (several, voi). And the negative with tu uses the infinitive, not the imperative.

EnglishItalian
Speak more slowly! (one person)Parla più lentamente!
Speak more slowly! (two or more)Parlate più lentamente!
Don't speak now!Non parlare adesso!
Take it!Prendilo!
Listen to me!Ascoltami!
  • Don't + verb with tu is non + infinitive: non parlare, non prendere. With voi the normal form stays: non parlate.
  • Take it, tell me: the pronoun is glued to the end of the verb, as one word: prendilo, dimmi.
  • Tone comes from your voice and per favore: the Italian imperative isn't rude. Entra, per favore is a friendly invitation.

The form

The Italian imperative has five persons: tu, noi, voi and two polite forms, Lei (one person) and Loro (several people). There is no io: we don’t give orders to ourselves.

Noi is English “let’s”: andiamo! (let’s go!), mangiamo qualcosa! (let’s eat something!). Loro is rare today (waiters, announcements, letters): with several people, in everyday life, Italians use voi. Verbs like finire keep -isc- as in the present: finisci, finisca, finiscano.

The trick: two families

You don’t need to learn a new table: the forms come from two tenses you already know.

Tu, noi, voi: same as the present
prendi · parti · parliamo · parlate
Lei, Loro: same as the subjunctive
(che) Lei entri → Entri! · (che) Loro aspettino → Aspettino!
Tu and Lei swap their vowels
tu parla ↔ Lei parli · tu prendi ↔ Lei prenda

Present tense among friends, subjunctive for ladies and gentlemen. Tu, noi and voi are the same as the present tense (tu prendi → prendi!); the only exception is tu with -are verbs, which keep their a: parla! Lei and Loro are the same as the present subjunctive: think of che Lei entri and drop the che. So the a and the i change places: tu parla, Lei parli; tu prendi, Lei prenda.

The negative

To tell someone not to do something there is just one special rule, and it is about tu.

Tu: non + infinitive
Non parlare! · Non correre! · Non partire!
Noi, voi, Lei, Loro: non + the same form
Non parliamo! · Non correte! · Signora, non parta!
With a pronoun: at the end or before
Non toccarlo! · Non lo toccare!
With Lei: the pronoun always before
Non si preoccupi! · Non lo dica a nessuno!

Non parla! is not an imperative: it means “he doesn’t speak”. “Don’t” + verb with tu is always non + infinitive: non parlare, non prendere, non fare. With a pronoun you can choose: non toccarlo or non lo toccare (don’t touch it), both correct.

Irregular verbs

The trick works here too: Lei and Loro take the subjunctive (vada, faccia, dica, venga, esca), noi and voi are almost always the present. There are few surprises.

The five short forms with tu: va’, da’, fa’, sta’, di’, to repeat like a nursery rhyme. For the first four the present works too: vai, dai, fai, stai. Essere, avere, sapere surprise you with tu and voi too, because they come from the subjunctive: sii, abbi, sappi; siate, abbiate, sappiate. Sii paziente! = “be patient!”.

With pronouns

With tu, noi and voi the pronoun is glued to the end, in one word, a bit like English “take it” written together; with Lei and Loro it goes before the verb.

Tu, noi, voi: the pronoun at the end
Prendilo! · Ascoltami! · Mandatela! · Prendiamolo!
After the short forms the consonant doubles
dimmi · fallo · dammi · vacci · stammi bene
Two pronouns together
Dammelo! · Portatemela! · Diciamoglielo!
Reflexive verbs
Alzati! · Sbrighiamoci! · Accomodatevi! · Si accomodi!
Lei and Loro: the pronoun before
Il modulo? Lo firmi qui. · Mi dica!

Di’ + mi = dimmi (tell me), fa’ + lo = fallo (do it), da’ + mi = dammi (give me): after the five short forms the pronoun’s consonant doubles. The only exception is gli: digli, fagli. With two pronouns (dammelo, give it to me) you use the forms of the lesson on combined pronouns. The stress doesn’t move: prendi → prendilo, mandate → mandatela.

The traps

Five points where many learners go wrong, and some spoken words you will hear all the time.

One person: Lei; several people: voi
Signora, entri! · Ragazzi, entrate!
Va’ (imperative) or va (present)?
Va’ a casa! (tu) · Marco va a casa. (lui)
Imperatives that became conversation words
Senti, … · Guarda, … · Dai, andiamo! · Scusa! · Mi raccomando!
Softer: a question in the present
Passami il sale. · Mi passi il sale?
The short forms with pronouns
Dimmi tutto! · Digli la verità!

Someone you address as Lei doesn’t get entrate: voi is for several people, Lei needs entri. English “you” hides this difference, Italian doesn’t. Va’ with an apostrophe is the imperative; va without it is “he goes”. Many imperatives have become conversation words: senti and guarda (“listen”, “look”) to start a sentence, dai! (“come on!”), mi raccomando (“don’t forget”). And to ask more gently, Italians often use a question in the present: mi passi il sale? (“could you pass me the salt?”).

Typical mistakes English speakers make

Wrong: Non parla adesso! Correct: Non parlare adesso!
Don't speak with tu is non + infinitive; non parla means “he doesn't speak”.
Wrong: Lo prendi! Correct: Prendilo!
Take it: with tu the pronoun is glued to the end of the imperative.
Wrong: Parlate più lentamente, signora. Correct: Parli più lentamente, signora.
One person you call Lei: parli; voi is for several people.
Wrong: Dimi la verità! Correct: Dimmi la verità!
Di' + mi: after the short forms the consonant doubles.
Wrong: Si siedi! Correct: Siediti!
Sit down with tu: the reflexive pronoun goes at the end, siediti.
